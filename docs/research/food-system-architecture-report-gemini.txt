Structural Architecture of Subsistence
Systems in Agent-Based and
System-Dynamics Simulations
Integrating dynamic food webs into socio-ecological agent-based models (ABMs) and
system-dynamics simulations presents a foundational computational challenge: the simulation
engine must accommodate biological renewal, trophic dependencies, spatial dispersion,
handling and search trade-offs, and delayed-return agricultural transitions without necessitating
structural rewrites of its core scheduling loops. A comprehensive review of models across
computational archaeology, evolutionary anthropology, macroecology, and systems-driven
colony simulations reveals standard mathematical formalisms and software patterns that permit
seamless extensibility.

Comparative Analysis of Prehistoric Subsistence
Simulators
Artificial Anasazi and the Village Ecodynamics Project
The Artificial Anasazi model was conceived to explain the demographic expansion and abrupt
regional abandonment of the Kayenta Anasazi within Long House Valley, Arizona, between AD
800 and 1300. Its subsistence sub-model is structured strictly around dry-farming of maize
across a cellular lattice where each cell represents a one-hectare plot.
Maize paleoproductivity is reconstructed exogenously on an annual cycle by integrating
retrospective dendroclimatological data—specifically the Palmer Drought Severity Index
(PDSI)—with localized soil geomorphological classifications. The environmental model converts
annual PDSI variations into five discrete harvest productivity tiers (659 to 1{,}201\text{ kg/ha}).
Household agents cultivate a selected cell and extract an annual yield calculated as:
Y_{\text{harvest}} = Y_{\text{potential}} \times U_{\text{factor}} \times \epsilon_{\text{stochastic}}
where U_{\text{factor}} = 0.64 denotes the proportion of potential harvest obtainable using
prehistoric agricultural methods, and \epsilon_{\text{stochastic}} introduces location-specific
noise.
Labor allocation is represented implicitly: households commit whatever labor is necessary to
their single selected hectare. Each household (fixed at five individuals) requires 160\text{ kg} of
maize per person annually, yielding an annual baseline consumption of 800\text{ kg} (560\text{
kcal/person/day}). Surplus grain is placed into granary storage with a hard two-year expiration
window; unconsumed grain older than two years rots completely.
The model lacks dynamic resource depletion: continuous cropping causes zero soil nutrient
exhaustion or yield degradation. Instead, mobility is purely reactive. Each April, if the
household’s remaining grain store plus the anticipated harvest (estimated directly from the
preceding year's yield) falls below the 800\text{ kg} annual requirement, the household
relocates. The relocation algorithm searches for an unoccupied, unfarmed cell within
1{,}600\text{ meters} of a persistent domestic water source that promises a yield of at least
160\text{ kg/person}. Household fissioning occurs probabilistically
([span_5](start_span)[span_5](end_span)[span_18](start_span)[span_18](end_span)p \in
[0.125, 0.155]) once accumulated corn reserves exceed defined thresholds, endowing the
daughter household with one-third of the parent store.
The Village Ecodynamics Project (VEP) expanded this architecture into the central Mesa Verde
region of southwestern Colorado and the northern Rio Grande of New Mexico, fundamentally
modifying the subsistence structure by embedding wild resource depletion and animal protein
requirements. The VEP modeled four coupled resource categories alongside maize: mule deer
(Odocoileus hemionus), lagomorphs (black-tailed jackrabbits, Lepus californicus, and desert
cottontails, Sylvilagus audubonii), fuelwood, and water.
Deer populations were implemented as dynamic stocks across 200\text{ m} \times 200\text{ m}
grid cells, governed by logistic regrowth functions tied to climatically fluctuating carrying
capacities, with spatial diffusion dispersing deer into underpopulated contiguous patches.
Hunting was modeled through central-place foraging principles: households harvested deer
within concentric travel radii, incurring travel costs and resource depression.
As human settlements aggregated into large villages, local deer herds were extirpated, creating
high energetic search costs. This depression drove two subsistence shifts: "garden hunting" of
pest lagomorphs attracted to cleared agricultural plots, and the domestication of the wild turkey
(Meleagris gallopavo). Turkey husbandry formed an endogenous feedback loop: turkeys
provided a critical protein buffer against game exhaustion, but because domesticated turkeys
were fed harvested maize, domestic protein production directly inflated human agricultural
acreage demands, accelerating forest clearance and regional deer habitat loss.
In both iterations, system calibration revealed severe sensitivities. Artificial Anasazi suffered
from rigid demographic oscillations: during protracted wet periods, populations expanded
exponentially until a sudden dry interval triggered a catastrophic Malthusian die-off. In the VEP,
early iterations produced runaway game collapse: because human populations were
energetically sustained by maize agriculture, their hunting pressure was disconnected from prey
availability. Human hunters hunted deer to landscape-wide extinction within decades. The
authors rectified this instability by introducing spatial terrain friction, travel energetic
expenditures, search limits, and inaccessible upland refugia.
In retrospective analyses, researchers noted that future iterations should incorporate flexible,
multi-household exchange networks, soil nutrient degradation (specifically nitrogen depletion
from continuous cropping), and endogenous household labor scheduling rather than static,
rule-based annual triggers.

HUMLAND Simulation Suite
The HUMLAND framework (spanning HUMLAND 1.0 through HUMLAND 2.0) was developed
by Scherjon, Nikulina, and colleagues to quantify the environmental transformations wrought by
prehistoric hominins (Neanderthals and Early Holocene Mesolithic hunter-gatherers) across
Europe. Operating on a coarse continental grid (10\text{ km} \times 10\text{ km} cells),
HUMLAND couples paleoclimatic vegetation simulations with hominin behavioral disturbance.
Resources are represented across two primary ecological tiers: standing vegetation biomass
and herbivore animal stocks. Vegetation is initialized using CARAIB dynamic global vegetation
models and calibrated against continental fossil pollen reconstructions (the REVEALS model).
Each grid cell tracks Net Primary Production (NPP), canopy openness (ratio of open pasture to
closed forest), and dominant tree composition.
Herbivorous megafauna (e.g., straight-tusked elephants, aurochs, bison) act as intermediate
biological agents that consume a fraction of cell NPP (V_c), driving vegetation openness
upward through persistent browsing and physical trampling:
V_c = f(\text{Megafauna Density}, \text{NPP})
Human hunter-gatherers alter this landscape via two distinct labor mechanisms:
   1.​ Direct hunting, which depresses herbivore densities and indirectly reduces browsing
       pressure, permitting closed-canopy forest succession.
   2.​ Anthropogenic fire regimes, wherein forager bands set deliberate fires around campsites
       whenever canopy openness drops below an empirical visibility and mobility threshold.
Labor allocation is governed by environmental state thresholds rather than explicit marginal
caloric calculations. Bands operate from seasonal campsites, dispatching hunting and burning
parties within a defined foraging radius. Campsite relocation operates across multi-year and
decadal time steps, triggered when local megafauna biomass is exhausted below a viable
encounter rate or when regional vegetation closes completely. Agriculture is deliberately omitted
from HUMLAND, as the model isolates pre-agricultural landscape modification.
A major technical obstacle in HUMLAND was extreme equifinality. Different configurations of
natural wildfire frequencies, megafaunal browsing pressures, and human fire ignition rules
produced near-identical regional canopy openness profiles. The authors countered this
instability by coupling NetLogo with a genetic algorithm, executing thousands of automated
simulation runs to optimize parameter configurations against empirical pollen databases.
In their methodological evaluations, the authors highlighted that the simulation treats hominin
foraging phenomenologically: human agents set fires and hunt based on environmental states
rather than tracking energetic acquisition against metabolic survival requirements. They
emphasized that coupling demographic metabolic survival directly to local foraging yields would
offer a more robust foundation for future continental-scale simulations.

MASON and NetLogo Canonical Forager Models
Within general-purpose agent-based simulation toolkits, canonical models such as Epstein and
Axtell’s Sugarscape (and its multi-agent extensions in NetLogo and MASON) formalize the
baseline mathematics of resource consumption and spatial mobility. In the foundational
single-resource Sugarscape, the environment is a toroidal grid where each cell maintains a
sugar stock bounded by a local carrying capacity C_i.
Regrowth is governed by a constant replenishment rule:
S_i(t+1) = \min(C_i, S_i(t) + \alpha)
where \alpha is a fixed integer increment (commonly \alpha = 1). Extended variants implement
seasonal growback, partitioning the lattice into hemispheres that alternate between summer
(\alpha = 1) and winter (\alpha = 0.125 or zero renewal) across 50-tick intervals.
Agents possess vision ranges (v \in [1, 6]) and metabolic burn rates (m \in [1, 4]). At each time
step, an agent scans its von Neumann neighborhood out to distance v, identifies the unoccupied
cell with the highest sugar stock, relocates to that cell, harvests the entire stock instantaneously
(Y = S_i), and deducts its metabolism m.
When the model is expanded to two resources (sugar and spice), agent utility is modeled using
Cobb-Douglas production functions:
U(w_1, w_2) = w_1^{\frac{m_1}{m_1+m_2}} \cdot w_2^{\frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2}}
where w_1, w_2 represent internal stocks and m_1, m_2 represent metabolic requirements for
sugar and spice, respectively. Agents calculate the marginal rate of substitution to trade or
allocate movement toward the resource currently yielding the highest marginal utility.
Within the MASON framework, social-ecological models such as HouseholdsWorld and related
agro-pastoral simulations (e.g., Cioffi-Revilla et al., Hailegiorgis et al.) extended these spatial
principles into pastoralism and rain-fed farming. Hailegiorgis et al. modeled rural households
adapting to climate variability in semi-arid environments, formalizing resource allocation across
farming and herding options.
Pasture biomass renews via non-linear precipitation indices, while crop growth accumulates
based on soil quality q and soil moisture m:
G_t = G_{t-1} + \omega \cdot q \cdot m
where \omega is the crop-specific growth rate. Labor is partitioned dynamically: households
compare the expected economic return of herding versus farming and allocate their labor
endowment proportionately. Farming operates as a multi-stage operational calendar: land
preparation occurs upon the anticipated onset of seasonal rains, followed by planting, manual
weeding, and harvest when crops reach the Length of Growing Period (LGP).
The primary stability hazard in these canonical models stems from extreme sensitivity to
metabolic burn rates and localized depletion. In Sugarscape, if starting densities slightly exceed
the landscape's mean replenishment rate, catastrophic mortality cascades occur, wiping out
large proportions of the agent population before spatial clustering stabilizes.
In MASON agro-pastoral models, households experience maladaptation traps: if climate shocks
disrupt rainfall onset, households misallocate labor to early planting, exhaust their seed
reserves, and experience consecutive crop failures. Modelers highlighted that rigid,
perfect-information utility optimization fails under seasonal volatility; architectures require
bounded-rational heuristic rules and communal mutual-aid mechanisms to absorb
environmental stochasticity.

Gallagher et al. (2019): Cultural Evolution of Forager Mobility
Gallagher, Shennan, and Thomas developed an agent-based cultural evolutionary simulation to
investigate how resource depletion rates, environmental replenishment rates, and human
mobility coevolve. The model is implemented across a two-dimensional lattice of hexagonal
cells, with each simulation step representing one calendar year.
Each patch i is characterized by a continuous foraging quality variable q_i \in [q_{\min}, 1.0].
Resource replenishment follows an asymptotic regrowth curve penalized by human foraging
extraction:
q_i(t+1) = q_i(t) + r \cdot (1 - q_i(t)) - \lambda \cdot n_i(t)
where r is the intrinsic replenishment rate, \lambda is the per-capita depletion scalar, and n_i(t)
represents the number of family units co-occupying cell i during year t.
Labor allocation is undifferentiated: agents spend their full annual effort harvesting food within
their current cell. The total foraging yield of the cell is partitioned equally among all resident
agents:
f_i(t) = \frac{q_i(t)}{n_i(t)}
Food income f_i is non-cumulative: agents do not store food across years. If an agent's annual
food income falls below a subsistence threshold (f_{\min} = 0.17), the agent dies from
starvation. If income exceeds f_{\min}, the agent reproduces via fission with an annual
probability scaled linearly up to p_{\max} = 0.14.
Mobility is modeled as an evolving cultural trait rather than an optimal calculation. Each agent
possesses a genetically or culturally transmitted mobility parameter m \in [0.01, 0.99].
Approximately once per generation (25\text{ years}, mutation probability \mu = 0.04), m mutates
via a binomial process informed by the agent’s recent food income. When deciding whether to
move, the agent tests m against a random draw; if movement occurs, the agent evaluates
neighboring cells j using an attractiveness metric A_j that penalizes travel distance:
A_j = f_j^* - \eta \cdot d_{i,j}
where f_j^* is the expected food yield of the target cell, d_{i,j} is Euclidean distance, and \eta is
the movement cost scalar.
Gallagher et al. represented food production phenomenologically by simulating ecologies with
high replenishment rates (r) and low depletion rates (\lambda). The simulation revealed a
counter-intuitive evolutionary outcome: while high-replenishment, low-depletion environments
strongly selected for sedentary strategies (low m), this sedentism caused dense population
packing that drove per-capita food income down to the starvation threshold (f_{\min}).
The model demonstrated that the physical and nutritional stress observed in early sedentary
archaeological populations emerges endogenously from density-dependent competition without
needing to invoke pathogen loads or dietary imbalances.
A major calibration challenge was the stochastic volatility of multi-agent packing, which
frequently resulted in localized extinction vortices. The authors developed "Outcomes
Clustering" to classify the multi-parameter state space. They noted that real human groups
mitigate packing pressure through storage and dietary switching, which were excluded from
their stylized model.

Optimal Foraging Theory Models: Central Place Foraging, Ache, and
Hadza
Optimal Foraging Theory (OFT) ABMs formalize human subsistence through algorithmic
microeconomic optimization, primarily utilizing the Prey Choice Model (diet breadth), the Patch
Choice Model (Marginal Value Theorem), and Central Place Foraging (CPF).
In the Prey Choice Model, resources are ranked by post-encounter profitability:
\pi_k = \frac{E_k}{h_k}
where E_k is the net energy yield (kcal) and h_k is the handling time (pursuit, capture,
processing). An agent expanding its diet breadth from k-1 resources to incorporate resource k
does so only when:
\frac{E_k}{h_k} > \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \lambda_i E_i - C_s}{1 + \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \lambda_i h_i}
where \lambda_i is the encounter rate with resource i, and C_s is the energetic expenditure per
unit search time. The critical dynamic of this model is that the inclusion of a low-ranked resource
depends entirely on the encounter rates of higher-ranked resources, not on its own abundance.
Central Place Foraging (CPF) models adapt these principles to residential base camps.
Foraging trips incur a round-trip transit cost 2d/v (where d is distance and v is walking speed)
and transport load limitations. The net energetic return rate delivered back to the central
campsite from patch j is expressed as:
R_{\text{camp}, j} = \frac{E_j(t_p) - (2 d_j / v) \cdot C_{\text{transit}}}{2 d_j / v + t_p +
t_{\text{proc}}}
where t_p is time spent foraging in the patch, C_{\text{transit}} is travel metabolic expenditure,
and t_{\text{proc}} is central processing time. Under the Marginal Value Theorem, an agent
abandons patch j when its instantaneous marginal return rate drops to the average return rate
across the broader landscape.
Kim Hill, Marco Janssen, and colleagues translated these formalisms into agent-based models
parameterized by longitudinal field observations of Ache foragers in the subtropical forests of
eastern Paraguay.
The Ache model simulates mobile prey species (peccaries, pacas, armadillos, capuchin
monkeys) alongside stationary plant resources (palm hearts, wild honey, seasonal fruits).
Individual hunters search the landscape, but high-yield prey encounters trigger cooperative
pursuit: nearby hunters recruit peers to surround peccary sounders or chop out burrowed
armadillos.
Ache residential mobility is exceptionally high, with bands establishing new campsites almost
daily. The simulation captures the mechanism driving this mobility: behavioral prey depression.
When human foragers enter a forest tract, mammalian prey species flee or enter deep burrows,
causing the encounter rate \lambda_i within an 8\text{ km} walking radius to fall precipitously
within 24 to 48 hours. Foragers who stay at a campsite for a second consecutive night
experience diminished returns, making daily residential relocation the optimal strategy.
In contrast, models simulating Hadza foragers in the savannah-woodland environment of
northern Tanzania emphasize sexual division of labor and central-place transport bottlenecks.
Men pursue large, mobile game (ungulates, giraffes) using bow and poison arrows,
characterized by high return rates upon encounter but prolonged, high-variance search times.
Women systematically target sessile, low-ranked, but zero-variance underground storage
organs (tubers like Vigna frutescens) and baobab fruit.
Hadza CPF simulations show that carcass transport introduces structural discontinuities: small
game is transported whole, whereas large game must be butchered at the kill site, with meat
defleshed and bundled into "meat shirts" to maximize the ratio of edible protein to carrier transit
costs.
A chronic instability in pure OFT agent-based models is the "extinction spiral". Because agents
act as rational profit-maximizers, they focus intensive hunting effort on high-ranked prey items,
exterminating local animal populations before switching to alternative prey. The authors resolved
this by incorporating prey flight dynamics, spatial refugia, search costs that scale inversely with
vegetation density, and stochastic encounter functions that prevent absolute resource stripping.

Pleistocene Overkill Simulations (Alroy 2001)
John Alroy formulated a multispecies macroecological system-dynamics model to evaluate
whether human hunting alone could account for the terminal Pleistocene extinction of 41 large
herbivore species (M > 44\text{ kg}) across North America.
The simulation operates across a grid of geographic cells covering North America. Each prey
species j reproduces logistically according to allometrically scaled biological constants:
\frac{dN_j}{dt} = r_{m, j} N_j \left( 1 - \frac{\sum_{k} \alpha_{jk} N_k}{K_j} \right) - P_j
where r_{m, j} is the intrinsic rate of increase, calculated directly from adult body mass M_j:
r_{m, j} = \exp(1.4967 - 0.370 \cdot \ln(M_j))
Carrying capacity K_j is determined by species biomass requirements, and \alpha_{jk}
represents interspecific competitive interactions among herbivores.
The human population H reproduces logistically toward a regional demographic saturation
density. Total human hunting predation on species j is governed by a multi-species non-linear
functional response:
P_j = H \cdot c_{\max} \cdot \frac{w_j N_j}{\sum_{k}
w_[span_250](start_span)[span_250](end_span)[span_255](start_span)[span_255](end_span)k
N_k + D} \cdot \min\left(1, \frac{\sum_k w_k N_k}{S}\right)
where c_{\max} is the maximum annual per-capita meat consumption, w_j is the hunting
vulnerability or preference coefficient, D is a half-saturation constant, and S is a low-density
threshold below which total hunting effort declines due to prey scarcity.
Alroy’s model exhibited catastrophic sensitivity to demographic decoupling. Because human
population growth was sustained by broad-spectrum foraging and alternative prey, human
numbers remained high even when slow-breeding megafauna (e.g., proboscideans, with r_m
\approx 0.06) collapsed. As a result, the model predicted rapid, sweeping mass extinctions
within 1,200 to 1,500 years of human entry.
Critics (e.g., Donald Grayson) demonstrated that the model’s catastrophic extinctions were an
artifact of parameterizing unrealistically low intrinsic growth rates (r_m) and omitting prey
switching thresholds. To stabilize the model and prevent the extermination of every large
mammal, Alroy had to enforce low maximum hunting capacities (c_{\max}) and introduce
low-density hunting cutoff parameters.
In retrospectives, researchers noted that overkill simulations must incorporate dynamic prey
evasion behaviors, explicit technological investment costs, and dynamic geographical barriers to
avoid artificial "blitzkrieg" collapses.

Colony Simulation Games with Documented Internals
Commercial simulation games model human-environment food systems using data-driven
software architectures that mirror academic ABM requirements while emphasizing runtime
efficiency.
Dwarf Fortress decouples entity behaviors from the underlying game loop by driving all plant,
animal, and dietary mechanics through plain-text configuration files ("raws"). Plants are declared
via [OBJECT:PLANT] tokens. Growth cycles are governed by integer tick counters rather than
continuous differential equations. The token [GROWDUR:n] specifies the duration in hundreds
of ticks required for a plant to reach harvestable maturity (with 1,008 units constituting a
standard game season).
Seasonal compatibility is defined using boolean tokens ([SPRING], [SUMMER], [AUTUMN],
[WINTER]). Structural components are declared via [GROWTH:NAME] tokens that have
independent scheduling scripts ([GROWTH_TIMING]). For example, a chestnut tree has distinct
growth tokens for its trunk wood, foliage, flowers, and edible nuts, with nuts configured to
mature and drop to the forest floor ([GROWTH_DROPS_OFF]) during late autumn.
Agriculture is spatial: farm plots must be designated on subterranean mud/soil or cleared
surface biomes. Seeds are itemized, physical entities stored in containers. If a dwarf consumes
a plant raw or brews it into alcohol, the seed remains intact and returns to the inventory; if the
plant is cooked in a prepared meal, the seed is destroyed. Labor allocation relies on a priority
job-dispatch queue: idle agents claim open planting, harvesting, or foraging jobs based on
fort-wide labor assignments.
In Songs of Syx, the landscape maintains continuous 2D scalar fields representing elevation,
soil composition, and ground moisture. A cellular automaton models groundwater diffusion
outward from sweet-water bodies, altering adjacent tile fertility dynamically. Farm plots are
spatial polygons where seasonal crop yields scale with base fertility, water-table proximity,
allocated workforce headcounts, and crop-specific phenology.
The game models agricultural labor across four phases: spring sowing, summer
tending/weeding, autumn harvesting, and winter fallow. Spoilage is implemented as a
continuous exponential decay function influenced by warehouse insulation, environmental
humidity, and technology upgrades. If winter arrives before fields are harvested, unharvested
crops rot in place, producing systemic starvation cascades.
Banished structures food production around central-place gathering and farming zones.
Gatherer Huts employ workers who search a fixed radius of natural forest canopy, harvesting
four wild food items (berries, mushrooms, roots, onions) that regenerate based on canopy
maturity. If a forester cuts down mature trees, gathering yields drop to zero until tree cover
regenerates.
Farming requires cleared, tilled fields. Crops follow rigid temperature and seasonal thresholds:
workers sow seeds in early spring, and crops accumulate yield toward maturity. If an early
autumn freeze occurs before workers transport the harvest to storage barns, standing crops
suffer frost kill tile-by-tile, permanently destroying that year's yield.
In Dawn of Man, wild food resources follow strict phenological windows. Wild cereals and tree
nuts can only be harvested during a brief window in late autumn, requiring specialized tools
(bone or flint sickles). Game animals possess sensory radii and flight routines. Intensive hunting
around a base settlement generates behavioral depression, causing animal herds to flee off the
local map edge and forcing hunters to travel farther. This depression provides the primary
incentive to domesticate cereals and pastoral livestock (goats, sheep, cattle) during the
Neolithic transition.
Across all four games, the most frequent failure mode is the "autumn harvest bottleneck":
because wild nuts, wild cereals, and domesticated crops mature simultaneously in late autumn,
agent labor queues become oversubscribed. Without heuristic priority overrides, agents
prioritize secondary tasks, leaving standing crops to rot in the field and triggering winter
famines.

Recurring Architectural Patterns in Foraging and
Farming Simulations
Synthesizing these diverse modeling traditions reveals five consistent socio-ecological patterns
that govern the design of extensible food systems.

Trophic Decoupling and the Protein-Calorie Scissor
When human agents obtain baseline metabolic calories from high-density, reliable plant
resources—such as wild grass seeds, domestic maize, or manioc—their demographic density
becomes detached from local game animal biomass. Because human starvation no longer acts
as a negative feedback loop to curb hunting, human hunters continue harvesting large game
even as animal densities collapse toward extinction. To avoid this runaway collapse, simulations
must implement a dual-currency nutritional architecture: tracking caloric requirements separately
from essential macronutrients (such as animal proteins or lipids).
This dual-currency dynamic recreates the historical subsistence transition observed in the VEP:
as deer were overharvested, human populations did not starve, but rather encountered a protein
bottleneck that forced them to domesticate turkeys and trade surplus carbohydrate calories to
sustain animal husbandry.

Search-Limited versus Handling-Limited Exploitation
Subsistence resources occupy distinct operational regimes along Holling Type II functional
response curves. Wild terrestrial game is overwhelmingly search-limited: finding an animal
requires significant time and energetic expenditure, whereas pursuit, butchery, and consumption
(handling) take relatively little time. The return rate for search-limited resources is therefore a
steep function of prey density per unit area.
Conversely, wild grains, mast nuts, and domesticated crops are handling-limited: locating the
patch requires trivial effort, but cutting, winnowing, hulling, grinding, or cooking impose heavy
linear time investments per unit mass. An extensible simulation engine must decouple search
efficiency (\beta_s) from handling cost (h_r); treating all food extraction as uniform gathering
creates models in which agents spend weeks "searching" for standing fields of wheat or catch
fleeing game with constant gathering times.

Phenological Pulsing versus Continuous Biological Renewal
While animal populations (small game, fish) reproduce across multi-season schedules that can
be approximated by continuous logistic differential equations, sessile plant resources exhibit
sharp, synchronous phenological pulses. Mast tree nuts (acorns, hickory) and wild grains
mature within a narrow seasonal window in autumn, followed by complete vegetative dormancy
during winter and spring.
If an ABM models wild cereals or nuts as continuous stocks that regenerate smoothly each time
step, agents will forage on them evenly year-round, failing to capture the intense autumn labor
competition that historically drove storage innovations and sedentary settlement patterns.

Sedentism Traps and Demographic Ratchets
As demonstrated by Gallagher et al. and the VEP, sedentism is rarely adopted because it offers
superior leisure or nutrition. Instead, environments with high resource replenishment and
predictable returns select for reduced mobility. Once a human group establishes a permanent
residential base, birth intervals decrease and population densities increase.
This demographic growth quickly depresses wild game within walking distance, pushing the
group's per-capita caloric intake back to the physiological margin (f_{\min}). Once this occurs,
returning to a mobile foraging lifestyle is impossible: the regional population has expanded far
beyond the carrying capacity of wild game, locking the society into labor-intensive agriculture
and local landscape modification.

The Delayed-Return Problem of Agriculture
Wild foraging operates as an immediate-return system: labor expended today yields food
consumed today. In contrast, agriculture is a delayed-return system: labor invested in spring
(field clearing, plowing, sowing) produces zero caloric payoff until autumn.
If an agent's labor allocation is governed purely by instantaneous optimal foraging theory (such
as the Marginal Value Theorem), agents will never choose to farm, because the instantaneous
marginal return of planting seed is negative. A simulation engine must either evaluate
agricultural tasks using anticipated discounted utility over an annual planning horizon or enforce
rule-based task partitioning that prioritizes agricultural calendars ahead of opportunist wild
foraging.

Modular Resource Architecture for Seasonal Grid
Simulations
To permit a simulation to initialize with two baseline resources (e.g., small game and wild grain)
and subsequently integrate fish, mast tree nuts, and domesticated crops without refactoring the
core scheduling engine, the software architecture must decouple three domains:
  1.​ The environmental grid patch state.
  2.​ The biological renewal and spatial diffusion strategy of the resource.
  3.​ The labor extraction and nutritional delivery mechanics.

Formal Mathematical Architecture
Let the simulation space be a regular 2D grid of patches c \in \Omega, updated across discrete
seasonal steps t \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\} (Spring, Summer, Autumn, Winter). Each cell tracks a vector of
resource biomass stocks \mathbf{S}_c = [S_{c, 1}, S_{c, 2}, \dots, S_{c, K}].

Biological State Update

At the beginning of season t, before agent harvesting occurs, each resource stock updates
according to local biological growth, spatial diffusion, and environmental carrying capacity:
S_{c, k}(t+1) = \operatorname{Clamp}\Big( S_{c, k}(t) + \Delta_{\text{bio}}(S_{c, k},
\mathbf{E}_c, t) + \Delta_{\text{diff}}(S_{\cdot, k}) - \sum_{a \in A_c} Y_{a, c, k}(t), \, 0, \, K_{c, k}
\Big)
𝑤ℎ𝑒𝑟𝑒$𝐸𝑐$𝑖𝑠𝑡ℎ𝑒𝑎𝑏𝑖𝑜𝑡𝑖𝑐𝑒𝑛𝑣𝑖𝑟𝑜𝑛𝑚𝑒𝑛𝑡𝑎𝑙𝑐𝑜𝑛𝑡𝑒𝑥𝑡(𝑡𝑒𝑚𝑝𝑒𝑟𝑎𝑡𝑢𝑟𝑒, 𝑠𝑜𝑖𝑙𝑚𝑜𝑖𝑠𝑡𝑢𝑟𝑒, 𝑝𝑟𝑒𝑐𝑖𝑝𝑖𝑡𝑎𝑡𝑖𝑜𝑛), $𝐾𝑐,𝑘$𝑖𝑠𝑡ℎ𝑒𝑝𝑎𝑡𝑐ℎ𝑐𝑎𝑟𝑟𝑦
\Delta_{\text{diff}}(S_{\cdot, k}) = D_k \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(c)} (S_{j, k} - S_{c, k})
For sessile plant resources (wild grain, nuts, crops), the diffusion coefficient D_k = 0. For mobile
game and riverine fish, D_k > 0, modeling dispersal from overcrowded or undisturbed patches
into depleted territory.
Biological renewal \Delta_{\text{bio}} varies by resource class:
   ●​ For continuously breeding stocks (small game): \Delta_{\text{bio}} = r_k(t) \cdot S_{c, k}(t)
       \left( 1 - \frac{S_{c, k}(t)}{K_{c, k}} \right)
   ●​ For seasonally pulsed wild vegetation (wild grains, mast nuts): \Delta_{\text{bio}} =
       \begin{cases} K_{c, k} \cdot \Phi_k(\mathbf{E}_c), & \text{if } t = \text{FlushSeason}_k \\
       -\mu_{\text{decay}} \cdot S_{c, k}(t), & \text{if } t \neq \text{FlushSeason}_k \end{cases}
       where \mu_{\text{decay}} represents seed drop, rot, and consumption by non-human
       wildlife.

Extraction Functional Response

When agent a allocates L_{a, k} labor hours to resource k in patch c, the extracted yield Y_{a, c,
k} accounts for search efficiency, handling time, and co-forager interference:
Y_{a, c, k} = \frac{\beta_{s, k} \cdot S_{c, k} \cdot L_{a, k}}{1 + \beta_{s, k} \cdot h_k \cdot S_{c,
k} + \sum_{b \in A_c \setminus \{a\}} \gamma_k \cdot L_{b, k}}
where \beta_{s, k} is search efficiency, h_k is handling time per unit biomass, and \gamma_k
captures mutual interference between multiple foragers in the same cell.

Parameterization of Subsistence Archetypes
By implementing the RegrowthStrategy and HarvestStrategy interfaces, diverse resources are
integrated into the simulation engine cleanly:
   ●​ Small Game (Cottontails / Jackrabbits):
         ○​ RegrowthStrategy: Logistic regrowth with seasonal breeding pulses
             (r_{\text{spring}} = 0.45, r_{\text{winter}} = 0.05). Diffusion coefficient D = 0.20
             allows animals to migrate across cell boundaries.
         ○​ HarvestStrategy: Search-limited Holling Type II curve. High search time per
             encounter (\beta_s = 0.05), low handling time (h = 0.15\text{ hr/kg}).
         ○​ Nutrition: 1{,}400\text{ kcal/kg}, 210\text{ g protein/kg}, 50\text{ g fat/kg}. High
             storage perishability (0.90\text{ season}^{-1}).
   ●​ Riverine / Coastal Fish:
         ○​ RegrowthStrategy: Zero capacity on land cells; high capacity on river/coastal cells.
             Sharp spring spawning pulses simulating anadromous runs.
         ○​ HarvestStrategy: Central place harvesting with fixed spatial infrastructure (nets,
             weirs). Low search time once runs occur; moderate handling/cleaning time (h =
             0.40\text{ hr/kg}).
         ○​ Nutrition: 1{,}200\text{ kcal/kg}, 180\text{ g protein/kg}, 60\text{ g fat/kg}.
   ●​ Mast Tree Nuts (Acorns / Hickory):
         ○​ RegrowthStrategy: Strictly pulsed mast renewal in Autumn based on summer
             rainfall. Zero growth in other seasons; unharvested nuts rot or are consumed by
             wildlife in winter (\mu_{\text{decay}} = 0.75). Diffusion D = 0.0.
         ○​ HarvestStrategy: Handling-limited extraction. Search time is negligible (\beta_s \to
             \infty); handling time is very high (h = 2.0\text{ hr/kg}) to account for gathering,
             hulling, and leaching.
         ○​ Nutrition: 4{,}500\text{ kcal/kg}, 60\text{ g protein/kg}, 450\text{ g fat/kg}. Low
             storage decay rate (0.05\text{ season}^{-1}).
   ●​ Wild Cereals (Wild Einkorn / Rice Grass):
         ○​ RegrowthStrategy: Autumn-only flush window, zero spatial diffusion. Seed dispersal
             mechanics: unharvested mature cereals shatter and drop their seeds if not
             collected during the autumn window.
         ○​ HarvestStrategy: Handling-limited harvest. High labor cost per kilogram for reaping,
             threshing, and winnowing (h = 2.5\text{ hr/kg}).
         ○​ Nutrition: 3{,}300\text{ kcal/kg}, 120\text{ g protein/kg}, 20\text{ g fat/kg}. Low
             storage decay (0.08\text{ season}^{-1}).
   ●​ Domesticated Field Crops (Maize / Emmer Wheat):
         ○​ RegrowthStrategy: State-machine delayed return. In Spring, planting consumes
             seed grain from household storage and initializes plot growth. In Summer, standing
             biomass accumulates as a function of temperature, precipitation, and accumulated
             weeding labor. In Autumn, biomass converts to harvestable grain. In Winter,
             unharvested standing crops are killed by frost.
         ○​ HarvestStrategy: Bulk area-clearance harvest. Labor is dedicated to field reaping
             and transport.
         ○​ Nutrition: 3{,}500\text{ kcal/kg}, 90\text{ g protein/kg}, 30\text{ g fat/kg}. Highly
             stable dry storage (0.05\text{ season}^{-1}).

Systemic Modeling Pitfalls and Calibration Failure
Modes
Overpredation and Trophic Collapse Cascades
In multi-species forager simulations, wild animals are frequently hunted to complete extinction
within decades because computational agents do not experience physical exhaustion and
possess complete information regarding animal locations. In real-world ecosystems, animals
respond to predation pressure with behavioral avoidance—fleeing, adopting nocturnal habits, or
retreating into rough terrain—long before their populations are biologically extirpated.
When behavioral avoidance is omitted, human foragers harvest prey down to the final individual.
Modelers must implement behavioral prey depression: patch search efficiency \beta_{s, k} must
decay exponentially as a function of cumulative hunting hours spent in that cell over preceding
cycles:
\beta_{s, k}(t) = \beta_{0, k} \cdot \exp\Big(-\psi \cdot \sum_{\tau=t-n}^{t-1} \text{Labor}_{c,
k}(\tau)\Big)
This formulation depresses harvesting returns, encouraging agents to relocate campsites or
switch to alternative resources while wild breeding stocks remain intact.

The Agricultural Labor Deadlock
Introducing crops into an agent-based simulation often results in agents refusing to farm. If
agent behavior is governed by short-term optimal foraging rules (such as daily or weekly
Marginal Value calculations), the instantaneous return of planting a seed in spring is zero, while
hunting or wild seed gathering provides immediate calories. As a consequence, agents starve in
winter because they fail to sow fields in spring.
Simulations must resolve this by modeling labor allocation through hierarchical goal
architectures: agents first satisfy seasonal baseline commitments (field preparation and
planting) before allocating remaining discretionary hours to opportunistic foraging.

Storage Decay Cliffs and Malthusian Catastrophes
In simulations tracking food storage (such as Artificial Anasazi), implementing hard expiration
cutoffs (e.g., grain disappears entirely after exactly 24 months) creates artificial demographic
cliffs. During prolonged wet intervals, households fill granaries to capacity, driving exponential
population growth. When drought occurs, all households consume their reserves at identical
rates.
At month 24, grain stocks across the entire region expire simultaneously, resulting in a sudden,
catastrophic population crash where thousands of agents die within a single time step. A more
robust design implements non-linear, continuous storage spoilage:
\frac{dS_{\text{stored}}}{dt} =
-\d[span_9](start_span)[span_9](end_span)[span_22](start_span)[span_22](end_span)elta_[spa
n_77](start_span)[span_77](end_span)1 \cdot S_{\text{stored}} - \delta_2 \cdot
(S_{\text{stored}})^2
The quadratic term penalizes excessive hoarding, accounts for pest infestations, and smooths
demographic adjustments during environmental downturns.

Labor Queue Deadlocks in Multi-Resource Flushes
When multiple resources mature in the same season—such as autumn mast flushes of nuts,
wild grains, and agricultural harvests—uncoordinated priority queues often break down. Agents
can become trapped in pathfinding routines or dedicate all labor to distant nut patches while
standing domestic crops rot in adjacent fields.
Simulations must implement spatial discounting and central-place transport costs directly into
utility prioritization, ensuring agents account for round-trip travel time, field clearing investments,
and storage proximity when ranking competing harvest tasks.

## Architectural Synthesis
Constructing an extensible subsistence simulation requires decoupling biological replenishment
from the agent decision loop. When resources are modeled as discrete implementations of a
unified interface, the core engine remains agnostic to whether an agent is pursuing mobile
game, harvesting an autumn acorn mast, netting an anadromous fish run, or weeding a
domesticated crop field.
Agents interact with abstract return-rate functions that evaluate search, handling, and
central-place transport costs. Concurrently, the spatial grid updates biological stocks according
to independent phenological and diffusion equations.
By separating search-limited extraction from handling-limited processing, tracking protein and
caloric currencies independently, and accounting for non-linear storage decay, a simulation
avoids the catastrophic collapse modes common in early socio-ecological models while
providing an expandable foundation for modeling human subsistence evolution.
