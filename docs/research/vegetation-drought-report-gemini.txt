1. Vegetation Decline During Drought
Vegetation decline operates on distinct timescales governed by rooting depth, tissue turnover,
and hydraulic architecture. In a seasonal-tick model (1 tick = 1 season / ~3 months), decline
rates differ across plant functional types.

Grassland & Steppe

  ●​ Decline Magnitude:
       ○​ Moderate Drought (1.0 \le \vert{}\text{SPEI}\vert{} < 1.5): Aboveground Net Primary
           Production (ANPP) drops by 20% to 35%.
       ○​ Severe to Extreme Drought (\vert{}\text{SPEI}\vert{} \ge 2.0): ANPP drops by 40%
           to 65% on average, with arid grasslands experiencing crashes up to 75% to 85%.
  ●​ Seasons to Nadir: 1 to 2 seasons (1–2 ticks). Grasslands track precipitation
     synchronously; production collapses in the first dry growing season.
  ●​ Data & Sources:
       ○​ Observed: Wilcox et al. (2024, Science, EDGE network, n = 170 grassland sites
           across 6 continents) showed a global mean 1-year severe drought caused a 35%
           ANPP reduction, with semi-arid grasslands declining by over 60%.
       ○​ Observed: Global meta-analysis by Gherardi & Sala (2015, PNAS, n = 13 LTER
           sites) confirmed direct, aridity-dependent sensitivity within 1 growing season.

Savanna & Shrubland

  ●​ Decline Magnitude:
       ○​ Herbaceous understory: Drops by 50% to 80% within 1 season.
       ○​ Woody/shrub component: Declines by only 10% to 25% under moderate drought
           and 30% to 50% under prolonged severe drought. Total standing forage falls by
           35% to 60%.
  ●​ Seasons to Nadir: 2 to 4 seasons (2–4 ticks). Deep taproots and woody storage buffer
     shrubs against short precipitation anomalies, but multi-season deficits deplete subsoil
     moisture.
  ●​ Data & Sources:
       ○​ Observed: Vicente-Serrano et al. (2013, PNAS, global satellite NDVI/SPEI analysis)
           found semi-arid shrublands show peak sensitivity at intermediate hydrological
           scales (6–12 months / 2–4 seasons).
       ○​ Observed: Ruppert et al. (2015, Global Change Biology, n = 8 semi-arid sites)
           demonstrated that shrub productivity resists single-season droughts but drops
            non-linearly if drought extends past 12 months.

Temperate & Boreal Forest

  ●​ Decline Magnitude:
       ○​ Moderate Drought: Canopy Gross Primary Production (GPP) drops 10% to 20%;
           stem radial growth falls 10% to 15%.
       ○​ Severe Drought: GPP falls 25% to 40%; stem radial growth falls 30% to 55%.
           Under extreme cavitation thresholds, canopy leaf shed reduces effective
           forage/biomass by up to 50%.
  ●​ Seasons to Nadir: 2 to 6 seasons (2–6 ticks). Forests buffer water stress using
     sapwood capacitance, deep root networks, and stomatal regulation; decline manifests
     over multiple seasons.
  ●​ Data & Sources:
       ○​ Observed: Ciais et al. (2005, Nature, n = 18 European flux tower sites) documented
           a 30% drop in temperate forest canopy GPP during the 2003 summer drought.
       ○​ Observed: Anderegg et al. (2015, Science, n = 1,338 tree-ring sites, 49,339
           site-years) observed stem radial growth reductions of 10–30% persisting across
           global forest biomes.

2. Recovery Timescales and Scaling
Recovery to \ge 90\% of pre-drought carrying capacity (K) is decoupled from decline rates. It is
controlled by the survival of plant meristems (bud banks), xylem hydraulic repair, and
carbohydrate reserves.
Drought Duration (D) ───► Grassland: 0.5× to 1.0× D (1–2 ticks)​
                              ───► Shrubland: 1.0× to 1.5× D (2–5 ticks)​
                              ───► Forest:          1.5× to 3.0× D (4–12 ticks)​

  ●​ Grassland / Steppe:
       ○​ Recovery Window: 1 to 2 seasons (1–2 ticks) for moderate droughts; 2 to 4
          seasons (2–4 ticks) after multi-year severe droughts.
       ○​ Scaling Factor: Typically 0.5\times to 1.0\times drought duration (D). If
          belowground root crowns survive, biomass flushes back within a single
          normal-rainfall season.
       ○​ Key Study: Wilcox et al. (2024, Science) found rapid resilience across 170 sites:
          ~80% of temperate grasslands recovered baseline ANPP within 1 post-drought
          growing season, showing minimal multi-year legacy effects unless the drought
          exceeded 3 years.
  ●​ Savanna / Shrubland:
       ○​ Recovery Window: 2 to 5 seasons (2–5 ticks).
       ○​ Scaling Factor: 1.0\times to 1.5\times D. Grass understory recovers in 1–2
          seasons, but woody browse regeneration and structural leaf rebuilding require
          multiple wet cycles.
  ●​ Temperate & Boreal Forest:
       ○​ Recovery Window: 4 to 12+ seasons (1 to 3+ years / 4–12 ticks). High-latitude
          boreal forests can require up to 4–5 years (16–20 ticks).
       ○​ Scaling Factor: 1.5\times to 3.0\times D.
        ○​ Key Studies:
             ■​ Schwalm et al. (2017, Nature, global remote sensing + FLUXNET synthesis)
                showed semi-arid grasslands recover in <6 months, while temperate and
                boreal forests take 12 to 28 months.
             ■​ Anderegg et al. (2015, Science) documented persistent "legacy effects"
                where wood growth remained suppressed for 2 to 4 years post-drought due
                to cavitation damage and carbohydrate starvation.

Scaling with Drought Length vs. Severity

  ●​ Duration dictates the exhaustion of belowground carbon stores and bud mortality.
  ●​ Severity (Vapor Pressure Deficit & Soil Water Deficit) dictates physical structural
     damage: xylem cavitation in trees, tiller death in grasses.
  ●​ Non-linear Transition: While recovery scales roughly linearly with drought length for
     moderate droughts, once severity crosses critical hydraulic or meristem thresholds,
     recovery time transitions into a multi-year structural rebuilding phase.

Tipping Points and State Changes

  1.​ Grassland-to-Shrubland Transition (Woody Encroachment):
        ○​ Mechanism: Severe multi-season drought (>2 consecutive years) kills perennial
           grass tussocks and their below-ground bud banks (Dalgleish & Hartnett, 2006, J.
           Ecology). When rain returns, fast-rooting woody shrubs (e.g., Artemisia, Prosopis,
           Larrea) capture deep infiltration before grasses can re-seed, permanently shifting
           the cell to shrubland.
        ○​ Threshold Conditions: Rainfall deficit >50\% for \ge 3 consecutive growing seasons,
           accompanied by grazing.
  2.​ Forest Die-off & Savannization:
        ○​ Mechanism: Hydraulic failure occurs when xylem water tension crosses the P_{50}
           or P_{88} threshold (water potential causing 50% or 88% loss of stem hydraulic
           conductivity; Choat et al., 2012, Nature).
        ○​ Consequence: Once canopy trees die, recovery does not take 1–3 years; it resets
           to secondary succession, requiring 20 to 100+ years (80 to 400+ ticks). The
           system shifts to open woodland, shrubland, or pyrophytic grassland.
  3.​ Desertification (Soil Erosion / Crust Destruction):
        ○​ Mechanism: Loss of plant cover below ~15–20% exposes bare soil to wind/water
           erosion, blowing away the organic A-horizon and creating surface crusting that
           prevents water infiltration (D'Odorico et al., 2012, Bioscience). The local carrying
           capacity K drops permanently unless restored by external soil development.

Grazing pressure during and immediately after drought acts as a strong compound stressor,
driving structural degradation.

  ●​ Exhaustion of Non-Structural Carbohydrates (NSC): Plants survive drought by storing
     starch/sugars in crowns and roots. Heavy defoliation during drought forces plants to burn
     root carbon reserves to respire, inducing plant starvation and tiller mortality.
  ●​ The "Green Flush Trap" (Post-Drought): When rains return, surviving herbivores
     concentrate on emerging green shoots. Repeatedly removing tissue before it can
     photosynthesize depletes remaining root reserves.
  ●​ Empirical Magnitudes:
        ○​ In semi-arid rangelands, heavy grazing during/post-drought delays biomass
           recovery by 2\times to 4\times (from 1–2 seasons up to 4–8 seasons) (Illius &
           O'Connor, 1999, J. Applied Ecology).
        ○​ In the Sahel and Australian arid zone, overgrazing during multi-year droughts
           lowered perennial grass basal cover by 70% to 90%, converting perennial
           grasslands into annual weedlands or bare soil (Fensham et al., 2010, Ecological
           Monographs, n = 43 long-term plots).

intrinsically slow demographic recovery.

Decline Dynamics

  ●​ Lag Time: 1 to 2 seasons (3 to 6 months / 1–2 ticks) after forage collapses.
       ○​ Reason: Herbivores initially maintain body condition by burning
           subcutaneous/visceral fat stores, decreasing activity, and shifting to lower-quality
           reserve forage (woody twigs, standing dry straw).
  ●​ Crash Point: Mortality spikes when dry-season protein falls below metabolic
     maintenance (~5–6% crude protein) or standing biomass drops below ~200–300 kg
     DM/ha. Juveniles and lactating females die first (tick 2), followed by prime-age adults
     during sustained droughts (ticks 3–4).
  ●​ Mortality Magnitude: Severe droughts cause 30% to 75% herd mortality.
       ○​ Serengeti Ungulates (1993 drought): Wildebeest population crashed by ~35% over
           1 year (Mduma et al., 1999, J. Animal Ecology).
       ○​ Amboseli / Mara (2009 drought): Zebra and wildebeest mortality reached 60% to
           75% across 3 consecutive failed rainy seasons (Ogutu et al., 2014, PLOS ONE).
       ○​ Red Kangaroos (Australia): Populations crash by 40% to 60% during ENSO
           droughts, with juvenile survival dropping to near zero (Caughley et al., 1987).

Recovery Dynamics

  ●​ Lag Time: 3 to 7 years (12 to 28 ticks / seasons) to return to ~90% of pre-drought
     abundance.
  ●​ Demographic Ceiling: Herbivores are constrained by biological parameters:
       ○​ Gestation periods: 7 to 9 months (bovids, equids, cervids).
       ○​ Litter size: Usually 1 calf/fawn per year.
       ○​ Age to reproductive maturity: 1.5 to 3 years.
       ○​ Maximum intrinsic rate of increase (r_{\max}): \approx 0.15 - 0.25 per year (4–6%
          per season).

Cache Lifespans & Spoilage Dynamics

Spoilage rates depend on moisture content, ambient temperature, microbial/fungal activity, lipid
oxidation (rancidity), and animal pests.
  ●​ Fresh Meat / Fish (Unprocessed):
         ○​ Warm Season (>15^\circ\text{C}): Spoils within 24 to 72 hours (maggots, bacterial
             decay).
         ○​ Sub-Zero / Boreal Winter: Lasts 1 to 2 seasons frozen in snow/permafrost caches.
  ●​ Dried / Smoked Meat & Fish (Biltong, Jerky, Split Salmon):
         ○​ Dry / Cool Climates: Lasts 3 to 9 months (1–3 ticks).
         ○​ Humid / Warm Climates: Lasts 1 to 3 months (<1 tick) before mold and dermestid
             beetle larvae consume the store.
  ●​ Pemmican (Dried pounded meat + rendered animal tallow in rawhide bags):
         ○​ Lifespan: 1 to 4 years (4 to 16 ticks). The rendered fat creates an anaerobic seal
             preventing moisture absorption and bacterial colonization. This was the premier
             multi-season buffer among subarctic/plains hunters.
  ●​ Nuts, Acorns, & Seeds (Acorns, Pinyon pine nuts, Mongongo nuts):
         ○​ Lifespan: 6 to 24 months (2 to 8 ticks) if stored dry in raised woven granaries or
             dry rock shelters.
         ○​ Vulnerabilities: Moisture induces lethal aflatoxin-producing molds (Aspergillus);
             rodents (packrats, mice) can consume >50\% of cached nuts within 1 season if
             structures are breached.
  ●​ Underground Tubers & Roots:
         ○​ Lifespan in Cache: 2 to 8 weeks (rot or sprout).
         ○​ Storage Strategy: Foragers deliberately left tubers unharvested in the ground
             ("living storage"), where they remain viable throughout multi-year droughts until
             excavated.
Buffer Sizes Held

  ●​ Equatorial & Arid Low-Latitude Bands (e.g., Ju/'hoansi San, Hadza):
       ○​ Buffer: 0 to 3 days. Storage was socially and ecologically maladaptive: mobility
          costs outweighed storage benefits, high temperatures spoiled provisions, and meat
          was shared immediately across the band to build social capital.
  ●​ Temperate / Boreal / Coastal Collectors (e.g., Nunamiut, Tlingit, Great Basin Paiute):
       ○​ Buffer: 1 to 2 seasons (3 to 6 months / 1–2 ticks). Storage was designed to
          bridge the non-productive winter/lean season, rarely to protect against multi-year
          famine. Multi-year personal caches were historically rare because storage
          infrastructure was difficult to protect against marauding predators, raiders, and
          mold.

Forager Responses to Drought: Mobility, Refugia, and Kin
Mobile foragers manage drought through spatial relocation, diet breadth expansion, and
inter-band social insurance rather than relying on stockpiled food.

Decision Timing (Seasons to Relocate)

  ●​ Initial Response (Tick 1 / Season 1): Foragers remain within their primary seasonal
     range but expand their dietary breadth. High-return game and choice berries disappear;
     bands switch to high-cost "famine foods" (fibrous tubers requiring extensive
     roasting/leaching, bitter melon, tree gum, lichen, unpalatable small game).
  ●​ Abandonment Threshold (End of Tick 1 to Tick 2 / 3–6 months): When daily energetic
     foraging returns drop below basal metabolic needs (~1,500–2,000 kcal/forager/day) or
     ephemeral surface water disappears, the residential camp moves. Bands do not wait to
     starve; relocation occurs rapidly.

Movement Distances

  ●​ Normal Seasonal Mobility: 5 to 25 km per residential move; home ranges encompass
     200 to 2,000 \text{km}^2.
  ●​ Drought Relocation:
       ○​ Arid environments (e.g., Kalahari, Australian Western Desert): Bands relocate 50 to
          300+ km across landscape borders (Gould, 1980; Kelly, 2013).
       ○​ Australian Western Desert Mardu and Pintupi recorded traversing journeys
          exceeding 250 to 400 km between distinct waterholes during multi-year droughts.

  1.​ Phase 1: Contraction to Known Refugia ("Key-Resource Points"):
        ○​ As ephemeral pans dry up, bands contract inward toward permanent water points
           (deep bedrock soakages, natural springs, perennial riverbanks).
        ○​ Bands that were dispersed across thousands of square kilometers aggregate in
           high-density "dry-season camps" around these refuges (e.g., the Ju/'hoansi around
           the 8 permanent Dobe waterholes; Lee, 1979).
        ○​ Trade-off: Forage and game around these permanent water sources are depleted
           rapidly within an 8–12 km walking radius, creating localized starvation conditions
           even though drinking water remains available.
  2.​ Phase 2: Long-Distance Kin Dispersal ("Social Storage"):
        ○​ If the drought persists past 2–3 seasons, high-density refuge aggregation collapses
           due to local depletion and social conflict.
        ○​ Bands activate regional social safety nets by calling in reciprocal gifting
           obligations (e.g., the hxaro system of the Ju/'hoansi; Wiessner, 1982, American
           Ethnologist).
        ○​ Mechanism: Individuals maintain gift-giving partnerships with distant kin 50 to 200
           km away in regions subject to different rainfall patterns. During extreme crises,
           families exercise their social right to migrate and live in their partners' territories until
           regional conditions reset.
        ○​ Simulation Application: If a cell's available water drops to zero, the band searches
           within its social network graph for allied cells with available resources; if none exist,
           it retreats directly to the nearest regional permanent water cell (Refugium).
Simulation Implementation Suggestions
To wire these dynamics into a seasonal tick engine (t = \text{season}):
# 1. Vegetation Dynamics (Logistic growth with drought penalty &
legacy)​
def update_vegetation(V, K_base, drought_severity, pft,
drought_duration):​
     # drought_severity: 0.0 (normal) to 1.0 (extreme)​
     # pft: 'grass', 'shrub', 'forest'​
     ​
     decay_rates = {'grass': 0.65, 'shrub': 0.35, 'forest': 0.15}​
     growth_rates = {'grass': 0.85, 'shrub': 0.40, 'forest': 0.10}​
     ​
     if drought_severity > 0.3:​
           # Rapid decline​
           V -= V * decay_rates[pft] * drought_severity​
     else:​
           # Logistic recovery throttled by post-drought legacy​
           legacy_drag = 1.0 / (1.0 + 0.2 * drought_duration)​
           r_eff = growth_rates[pft] * legacy_drag​
           V += r_eff * V * (1.0 - V / K_base)​
           ​
     return max(0.05 * K_base, V)​
​
# 2. Herbivore Population (Lagged response)​
def update_herbivores(H, V, K_veg):​
     forage_ratio = V / K_veg​
     if forage_ratio < 0.4:​
           # Starvation threshold crossed: mortality spike​
           mortality = 0.25 + 0.40 * (0.4 - forage_ratio)​
           H *= (1.0 - mortality)​
     else:​
           # Slow demographic rebuild (r_max ~ 5% per seasonal tick)​
           r_herb = 0.05​
           H += r_herb * H * (1.0 - H / (V * 0.2))​
     return max(0.0, H)​

This model mirrors the key dynamics documented above: grass plunges in a single tick and
rebounds in one to two; forests decline slowly and show long multi-tick recovery lags; herbivores
crash only after a one-to-two tick lag and need many ticks to recover; and human agents
migrate early toward water refugia and kin nodes rather than relying on stored food.
